# Before You Read the Qur'an (script, version 2)

What changed from version 1:
- It uses Islamic terms: Allah instead of God, the Prophet ﷺ, in sha' Allah, rahimahullah.
- It is told as a story to young Muslims, in short spoken lines. Each paragraph carries a voice cue in square brackets, the way the "How merciful is Allah" narration did.
- It ends by inviting the viewer to open Tafhim al-Qur'an.

Everything said about Mawdudi and his preface comes from the preface itself. Page numbers are in brackets for checking, and they are not spoken. Lines addressed to "you" are the video speaking, not the author.

About 1,300 words, which comes to roughly 9 to 10 minutes at a slow, warm pace.

---

## 1. The cell [p. 9] (style: woodcut)

[softly, slow] Multan. September 1949.

[softly] A prison cell. One small lamp. And a man bent over a page.

[warmly] His name is Sayyid Abul A'la Mawdudi, rahimahullah. He is writing the preface to a book he began seven years earlier. A book that would travel far beyond that cell.

[with quiet awe] Tafhim al-Qur'an. Making the Qur'an understood.

[gently] Before the book, he wrote a short preface. Only nine pages. Most people skip it. But in those nine pages, he tells you how to meet the Book of Allah. So let's not skip it.

## 2. Why one more? [p. 1] (style: paper-cut lightbox)

[thoughtful] He begins with an honest question. Shelves were already full of translations and tafsir. So why add one more?

[firm, sincere] Writing another one just for the blessing of it, he says, is not a good use of time anymore. It is only worth it if it fills a gap. A need that the earlier works left unmet.

## 3. The thirst [pp. 1–2] (style: paper-cut lightbox)

[tender] And he had seen that need for years. Ordinary, educated Muslims wanted to reach the spirit of the Qur'an. They wanted to know what this Book is really asking of them.

[softly] The translators had done valuable work. And still, he writes, that longing stayed thirsty.

[warm, rising] He felt he could help quench that thirst. And those two feelings, the thirst he saw and the help he could give, became this book.

[clear] So who is it for? Not the scholars. They already have plenty. It is for people like you and me. People who did not study Arabic. So he left out the debates that only specialists need.

[earnest] His aim was simple. That you understand what Allah is saying, clearly. And that you feel what the Qur'an wants you to feel. Where a doubt comes up, he answers it right there.

[humble] And then, with real humility, he asks his readers and the scholars: show me where I fell short. This is not the last word.

## 4. Why not word for word? [pp. 2–3] (style: ink on paper)

[curious] Then comes the heart of the preface. How do you carry the Qur'an into another language?

[measured] He chose a free rendering. He calls it tarjumani. Not because word-for-word translation is wrong. Great scholars had done it beautifully: Shah Waliullah in Persian, and in Urdu Shah Abdul Qadir, Shah Rafiuddin, Mawlana Mahmud al-Hasan, Mawlana Ashraf Ali Thanawi, and Hafiz Fateh Muhammad Jalandhari. Word for word teaches you what every word means. That is a real gift.

[gently] But for someone who doesn't know Arabic, he says, it has weaknesses. And the first one is life.

## 5. Life [pp. 3–4] (style: ink on paper)

[slow, heavy] You read a literal translation, and the words just lie there.

[softly] Your heart is not moved. The hair on your skin does not rise. No tears come. Nothing stirs inside you.

[quietly, almost a whisper] And a terrible thought creeps in. Is this really the Book that challenged the whole world?

[explaining, warm] He says a literal translation is like a sieve. The dry ingredients of the medicine pass through. But the living spirit of the language flies away, above it.

[building] And the Qur'an's power was in its language as much as in its teaching. That is what melted hearts of stone. That is what struck Arabia like lightning. Its fiercest enemies were afraid that whoever listened to it would lose his heart to it.

[softly, with weight] Had it come in the language of its translations, he says, it could never have done that.

## 6. Flow [p. 4] (style: one line)

[lighter] The second weakness is flow. Translations sit between the lines, or on half a page. Fine for study. But you cannot read them straight through, like a book. English is even worse. Every verse cut out and numbered.

[gently] Take any beautiful piece of writing, he says, and number its sentences. Half of its effect is gone.

## 7. Speech, not writing [pp. 4–5] (style: one line)

[with emphasis] And then a point he calls very important. The Qur'an is speech. Not writing.

[storytelling] It did not come down as a printed book. As the call to Islam moved forward, Allah sent down an address for each moment, and the Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam, delivered it to people like a khutbah.

[explaining] And speech works differently. On paper, you first write the objection, then the answer. In a speech, the objectors are standing right there. So the answer simply comes. On paper, you must describe the setting. In a speech, the setting is all around.

[wonder] And in a speech, the voice turns. People are spoken about, then spoken to. One person, then many. Sometimes the speaker speaks for himself, sometimes for a group, sometimes on behalf of Allah. And sometimes, Allah Himself speaks.

[softly] Heard aloud, this is beauty. On paper, it can feel disconnected. In Arabic, not one letter may be changed, so only notes can help. But in another language, if the speech is carefully turned into written style, the gap closes.

## 8. Setting [p. 6] (style: one line)

[thoughtful] Every surah came for a moment. It had a background, and a need it came to meet. Strip that away, and some things are not understood at all. Some are understood upside down. So a rendering should carry some of that background with it.

## 9. One word, six meanings [pp. 6–7] (style: copperplate engraving)

[intrigued] And the Qur'an has its own vocabulary. A language within the language.

[slow, one by one] Take one word. Kufr. In one place it means complete disbelief. In another, simple denial. In another, only ingratitude. In another, falling short in something faith requires. In another, believing in principle, but refusing in practice. And in another, obeying on the outside, while disbelieving inside.

[pointed] Translate it as kufr everywhere, and you are technically correct. But the reader will miss it here, misread it there, and get lost somewhere else.

## 10. Afraid [p. 7] (style: ink on paper)

[gentle resolve] So here is what he did. He did not dress the Qur'an's words in Urdu clothes. He read a passage. He took the meaning he understood, and the effect it left on his own heart. And he carried that, as faithfully as he could, into his own language.

[earnest] Clear Arabic should become clear Urdu. The flow of speech should come through. And the majesty of Allah's words should shine, as far as possible.

[slow, heavy] That needed freedom. But this was the Word of Allah.

[very softly, emotional] So he writes: I used this freedom... while very much afraid.

[quiet] And he never stepped beyond what the Qur'an's own words allow.

## 11. Two more tools [pp. 7–8] (style: watercolour journal)

[warm] What a rendering cannot carry, he gave you in two more ways. Before every surah, an introduction: when it came down, what was happening, where the call to Islam stood, and what it was facing. And notes, but only in two kinds of places. Where a question would rise in your mind. And where you might hurry past, and miss the real spirit of a verse. Nothing that pulls your eyes away from the Qur'an.

## 12. How to read [p. 8] (style: watercolour journal)

[inviting] And then he gives you a way to read. One. Read the surah's introduction, and come back to it as you go. Two. Take your daily portion, and first go through it word for word, verse by verse, in whatever language you like. Three. Read his rendering of that portion straight through, without the notes, so the whole message stands before you at once. Four. Go back, verse by verse, with the notes.

[warmly, reassuring] Read like this, he says, and an ordinary reader will come to know the Qur'an well. Maybe not like a scholar. But well, in sha' Allah.

## 13. The prison gave him time [pp. 8–9] (style: woodcut)

[storytelling] He began in February 1942. For more than five years he wrote, until he reached the end of Surah Yusuf. Then life left him no time to write, or even to revise.

[a small smile] Call it good luck or bad luck, he writes.

[slow] In October 1948, he was suddenly arrested and taken to jail. And there, behind the bars, he found the time the book needed.

[tender] He ends with a dua: that this work truly helps the servants of Allah understand the Qur'an. And one line in Arabic.

[reverent] Wa ma tawfiqi illa billah. My success is only through Allah.

## 14. Your turn (style: paper-cut lightbox)

[softly] A prison cell. One small lamp. And a book that travelled far beyond it.

[warm, direct] Now it is in your hands. Read the setting. Learn the words. Hear it whole. Then go deep.

[gentle, final] Open Tafhim al-Qur'an. Start with one surah. And let it reach your heart.
